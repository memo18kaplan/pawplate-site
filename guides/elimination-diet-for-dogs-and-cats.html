Elimination diet for dogs and cats: the 8-week plan

An elimination diet is the only dependable way to find out whether food is behind your pet's itch. It is simple on paper and hard in practice: one diet, nothing else, for about eight weeks. Here is how it works and where it usually goes wrong.

Why not just do an allergy test?

Blood and saliva tests sold for food allergy are not reliable in dogs and cats. They miss real reactions and flag foods the pet eats without trouble. Veterinary dermatologists use a diet trial instead: feed a diet the immune system has no history with, see whether the itch settles, then bring the old foods back one by one and see which one brings it back.

Step 1: choose the diet with your vet

The trial food is either a protein your pet has never eaten (a "novel" protein) or a hydrolyzed diet, where the proteins are broken into pieces too small for the immune system to recognize easily. Some vets also use a home-cooked diet with a single protein and carbohydrate.

Write down everything your pet has eaten before: foods, treats, chews and supplements. That list is how your vet picks a protein that is truly new. Many vets prefer prescription or home-cooked trial diets because they control exactly what is in the bowl.

Step 2: eight weeks, nothing else

A review of published trials found that about 8 weeks on the diet is needed to see improvement in more than 90% of food-allergic dogs and cats. Stopping after 3 or 4 weeks can make a trial look like a failure when food really was the cause.

During those weeks the diet has to be the only thing your pet eats. One slip can bring the itch back and cost you weeks. These are the usual culprits:

  • Treats, dental chews, rawhide and bully sticks
  • Table scraps, licked plates and food dropped on the floor
  • Flavored medicines: chewable flea, tick and heartworm tablets, flavored pastes
  • Food used to hide pills (cheese, peanut butter, ham)
  • Flavored toothpaste and supplements such as fish oil
  • Another pet's bowl, and for cats, anything caught outdoors

What to expect, week by week

WeeksWhat happens
1–2Switch to the trial diet (gradually, unless your vet says otherwise). Itch usually has not changed yet. Clear the cupboards of old treats.
3–4A good time to check in with your vet: skin and ear infections keep the itch going and may need treating on their own.
5–8If food is the cause, itch scores usually fall clearly over these weeks.
After 8Compare the last week with the week before the trial. A clear drop means it is time to re-introduce foods.

Step 3: re-introduce one food at a time

Improvement alone does not prove a food allergy: the itch might have eased for another reason, such as the season changing. The proof is the "challenge": add back one old food, keep everything else the same and watch. In studies most reactions showed up within 2 weeks in dogs and within 1 week in cats.

If the itch returns, stop that food and wait for it to settle before testing the next one. A flare that comes with a food and goes when you stop it points to a trigger. Foods that cause nothing can stay in the diet.

Keep a daily log

Eight weeks is long enough that memory stops being useful. Score the itch from 0 to 10 at the same time each day, note where it shows up, and write down every slip the day it happens. At the end your vet can see the trend at a glance instead of relying on "I think it is a bit better".

Frequently asked questions

How long does an elimination diet take?

About 8 weeks on the trial diet, then a few more weeks to re-introduce old foods one at a time. Most food-allergic pets improve within 8 weeks.

Can I give treats during an elimination diet?

Only treats made from the trial diet itself, or ones your vet approves. Pieces of the trial food or kibble from the same bag work well as treats.

What if the itch does not improve after 8 weeks?

First check the log for slips and untreated infections. If the trial was strict, food is less likely to be the main cause, and your vet will look at environmental allergy and other causes next.
